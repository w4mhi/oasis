Weather Radio

An always-on NOAA Weather Radio watch that decodes official SAME/EAS alerts off the air — no internet, no NWS API

NOAA Weather Radio broadcasts continuous forecasts on seven VHF channels between 162.400 and 162.550 MHz, and prefixes every official National Weather Service alert with a SAME header — a short burst of data naming the event, the issuing office, the counties affected, and when the warning was issued and expires. OASIS points an RTL-SDR at the strongest channel in your area, demodulates that header with multimon-ng, and parses it against county tables vendored in the repo. The alert reaches you by radio, exactly as it was designed to, with nothing on the internet involved.

⚠

The watch holds the RTL-SDR for as long as it runs. Only one consumer can own a dongle, so the weather watch is mutually exclusive with ADS-B, the APRS SDR feed, satellite listening and OpenWebRX on the same dongle. That is the trade: an always-on watch is only always on if it keeps the radio. A second dongle lets you run both.

Turning it on

⚠

There is no Listen button, and no START button. You start the watch by routing an RTL-SDR to nwr in the SRV OPS hardware console, and you stop it by taking the device away. See Service Operations. The service controls grant must be in place — the console uses the same sudoers rule.

Earlier versions worked the other way round: you opened the page and pressed Listen, and the capture lived inside the web server. That is gone, for two reasons. An alert you miss because nobody pressed a button is worth very little. And a capture that dies when the web server restarts, or does not come back after a reboot, is not a watch.

UnitRole
oasis-nwrThe watch. Sweeps the band, keeps the strongest channel, runs rtl_fm into multimon-ng continuously, records every decoded alert, and serves status, live audio and a retune notification on 127.0.0.1:8089. Written by the installer but left disabled — assigning the dongle is what starts and enables it, so the station returns after a power cut in the state you left it.

Install it from the setup menu (feature nwr) or directly: 

python3 services/nwr/install.py           # multimon-ng + the oasis-nwr unit
python3 services/nwr/install.py --serve   # run the watch in the foreground (dev)

What the watch does on its own

Left alone, it runs a loop you never have to touch:

  • Scan — a short rtl_power sweep of the 170 kHz band tells it which of the seven channels is actually on the air. A sweep is the right instrument here: measuring loudness on demodulated audio is not, because an empty FM channel demodulates to full-scale noise and reads as a strong signal.
  • Listen — it tunes the winner and decodes SAME continuously, writing each alert to the store as it arrives.
  • Rescan — periodically, and only while a capture is healthy, it re-checks that the channel it chose is still the best one. If the winner does not stand at least 2 dB above the median of the other six channels it says so (weak scan) and backs off to a slower rescan rather than churning the radio — but it listens on the best channel it found regardless. A weak band is a report, never a refusal to start.
  • Margin, not level — that 1 dB is a gap between channels, not a signal strength, because the strength on its own says nothing. Measured on this station against a live transmitter, the six empty channels read −5.7 dBm and the transmitter read −1.95 dBm: a margin of 3.75 dB. A second sweep minutes later, same antenna and same transmitter, gave 2.19 dB — the margin on one station moves by more than a decibel between sweeps, which is why the threshold sits well below the best reading rather than just under it. Pull the antenna off and all seven channels read the same noise, the margin falls to nothing, and the card goes amber. The card’s meter and sub-line show that margin.
  • Retry — if it cannot get the dongle it waits and tries again, with the reason visible on the page. It does not spin.

The Weather Radio page

Reachable from the dashboard card, at /server/nwr/. It starts nothing — the watch is a service, not something this page launches — and it does exactly five things.

  • Pin a channel. Choosing one from the dropdown overrides the automatic pick. The change takes effect while you watch: the page writes the setting and the server asks the daemon to re-read it. Selecting the channel already tuned interrupts nothing.
  • Listen in. The audio player carries the watch’s own audio, relayed byte-for-byte from the daemon — you are hearing the capture that is decoding, not a second one opened for you. Up to three listeners at once.
  • Read the decode log. Received time, event, affected areas, expiry and the issuing station, newest first.
  • Switch the bell on. The Spoken alerts card: one bell glyph, off until you click it. It shows which of its three states the station is in — off, speaking, or on but held silent by quiet hours — and carries the button that speaks through tonight’s quiet hours. See The bell below.
  • Choose your counties. The Your counties card: type two letters of a county name, a two-letter state, king, wa, or a FIPS code, and pick from what comes back. What is chosen shows as a row of chips; the × on one stops watching it. A county with no coordinates is drawn with a dashed border — it can be watched, it just cannot be plotted.
ℹ

A brief gap in the audio right after you pin a channel is the retune, not a fault. The page labels it as such and deliberately does not colour it as an error — the capture is down on purpose for a second or two while the daemon re-tunes.

Alerts, counties and the map

Each decoded header becomes a record carrying the raw SAME string, the event code and its plain-English name, the issuing office, the county FIPS list, the issue and expiry times, and a type used to colour it. Counties resolve to names and, where the table has coordinates, to points on the traffic map alongside APRS and ADS-B. A statewide code has no single point and is listed without a pin rather than dropped.

The watch list (watch_fips) is how you say which counties matter to you. It decides what the bell speaks — not what gets decoded; everything heard is recorded either way.

You set it on the Weather Radio page, in the Your counties card: type part of a county name and pick it from the list. A station watches a handful of counties, not the three thousand in the table, so it is built to be typed into rather than scrolled. The four SAME codes with no coordinates, and every marine zone, can still be watched by typing the code itself — an alert matters whether or not the map can draw it.

⚠

An empty watch list matches everything. That is the safe default: a new install acts on every alert it hears. A wrong list, by contrast, matches nothing and looks exactly like a dead receiver — the alerts still arrive in the log, but nothing is ever flagged.

The store keeps up to 500 records and sweeps ones that expired more than a week ago, but an alert that is still active is never evicted, whatever the budget says. A record whose timestamps came from a clock OASIS does not trust is marked clock_suspect and is not retired by its own expiry — see GPS Time & RTC if you see those.

The bell

Off by default: a watch that starts talking the moment you assign a dongle is a watch you turn off. The switch is the bell glyph in the Spoken alerts card on the Weather Radio page. Click it and matched alerts are spoken aloud through the station voice, honouring the station-wide quiet hours (22:00–07:00 local by default) with an override that expires by itself at the next window end — a stormy night is a night, not a change of policy.

The glyph has three appearances because the bell has three states, and two of them are easy to confuse: dim and crossed is off, ringing is speaking, and dimmed and crossed but still labelled on is the switch on with quiet hours holding it silent until 07:00. The card says which in words as well. Below it, Speak through quiet hours tonight sets the override; it is refused — visibly, in the server’s own words — if what the browser asks for would outlive the next window end, which is how a wrong clock shows up as an error instead of as a station that quietly stopped keeping quiet hours.

ℹ

There is no severity filter, on purpose. Turning the bell on means you will also hear the Required Weekly Test. That weekly announcement is the only regular proof that demodulation, parsing, county matching and speech all still work — a station that has been silently broken for a month is worse than one that says something you did not need on a Wednesday morning.

The API

The web server is a reader and a byte relay. It has no encoder, no capture, and it never writes the alert store — the daemon is that store’s single writer, because two writing processes lose records under exactly the conditions that matter: several counties, several messages, close together.

EndpointReturns
GET /api/nwr/statusLocal preconditions (dongle assigned, busy, dependencies present), the stored config, the seven channels, and the watch’s own account of itself — reachable, phase, channel, alerts_seen, last_decode, scan_weak, retune_pending.
GET /api/nwr/alertsThe decode log, newest first, plus the ids that are still active.
GET / POST /api/nwr/configRead or write the settings. A write that moves the pin also asks the daemon to re-read them, and reports back whether it was reachable and whether the request was accepted.
GET /api/nwr/countiesThe SAME county table behind the watch-list picker.
GET /api/nwr/listen/streamLive audio, relayed from the daemon.
⚠

ok means the request succeeded, not that the news is good. “The watch is not running” is an answer: it comes back with ok: true and watch.reachable: false, with a detail saying why. Anything reading this endpoint must branch on reachable and phase, never on ok.

The capture-control routes of the first version — /api/nwr/listen, /api/nwr/listen/stop and /api/nwr/scan — are retired. A stale page asking for one gets a 405 (or 404 on a GET) and nothing starts, which is the point: a capture spawned from the web server would fight the daemon for the same dongle, and a server-side sweep would take the tuner away from a running watch for six seconds.

Troubleshooting

SymptomCause & fix
Dashboard card is grey / NOT INSTALLED Run python3 services/nwr/install.py, or tick nwr in the setup menu, then reload.
The card says no dongle is assigned Route an RTL-SDR to nwr in the SRV OPS console. That is the start action — there is no button anywhere else.
Status shows reachable: false The oasis-nwr unit is not running. systemctl status oasis-nwr, then journalctl -u oasis-nwr -e.
Phase sticks at retrying with an error about the dongle Something else holds the radio. Look for usb_claim_interface error -6 in the log — it appears within a fifth of a second of the spawn, so the process still “starts” successfully. Route the device away from the other consumer rather than killing it by hand.
Audio is weak or dead, everything reports healthy Check the log for Tuner gain set to 0.00 dB. That means zero gain, not automatic — rtl_fm reads -g with atof(), and atof("auto") is 0.0. Healthy reads Tuner gain set to automatic. Do not “fix” a hand-rolled command by adding -g auto; omitting the flag is the only way to ask for AGC.
scan_weak: true, nothing ever decodes No channel stands clear of the rest of the band — the card shows the measured margin, and it will be near zero. 162 MHz is line-of-sight to the transmitter: this is antenna, coax or siting, not software. A dongle whip indoors may hear nothing where a rooftop antenna hears the same station cleanly.
Audio is clear but no alert in days Most likely a quiet week. The Required Weekly Test is the proof the chain works — wait for it before suspecting the decoder.
Alerts are logged but the bell never speaks Either the bell is off, or the alert did not match your watch_fips, or it is quiet hours. All three are normal states, the Spoken alerts card on the Weather Radio page shows which of the three you are in, and the daemon’s log names it too.
The audio player will not open 409 = the watch is not capturing. 503 = no MP3 encoder installed (ffmpeg, or sox with libsox-fmt-mp3), all three stream slots busy, or the daemon is unreachable.
curl <pi-ip>:8089/status refuses the connection The daemon is loopback-only, like adsb-api. Run the curl on the Pi, or go through /api/nwr/status on :8083 from anywhere.

The full field-debug sequence, with the healthy and broken output for each command, is in docs/SETUP.md under NOAA Weather Radio (SAME/EAS).
